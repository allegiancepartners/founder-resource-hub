Appendix B — Basic Estate Plan

Illustrative NYC Founder  ·  Allegiance Partners  ·  2026

Overview

A foundational estate plan establishes the legal framework for asset transfer, incapacity management, and wealth preservation. For a founder in their mid-30s with significant anticipated liquidity from an upcoming exit, this baseline layer is essential before more complex structures — the Dynasty Trust, grantor trust elections, charitable vehicles — are layered on top. Allegiance recommends engaging a trusts & estates attorney from its Trusted Advisor Bench to complete the Basic Estate Plan.

Core Documents Required

Last Will & Testament
Directs disposition of probate assets; names executor. If the founder currently has no children, no guardian designation is required today — revisit if that changes.
Revocable Living Trust
Avoids probate for NY real property; coordinates with the Dynasty Trust and QSBS assets.
Durable Power of Attorney
Authorizes an agent to manage financial affairs during incapacity. Especially important for a sole founder.
Healthcare Proxy / HIPAA Authorization
Designates a healthcare decision-maker; HIPAA authorization for medical record access.
Living Will / Advance Directive
Documents end-of-life care preferences. Required under NY Health Care Proxy Law.

NY Estate Tax — The Cliff Effect

New York imposes an estate tax with a significant 'cliff effect': if the gross estate exceeds 105% of the NY exemption (~$7.16M in 2026), the entire estate — not just the excess — becomes subject to NY estate tax. This creates a punitive marginal rate that can exceed 100% on estates near the cliff threshold.
  • NY exemption (2026): ~$7,160,000
  • Cliff threshold (105% of exemption): ~$7,518,000 — above this, ALL assets are taxable, not just the excess
  • Rate on amounts above the exemption: ~16%
  • Planning action: use the Dynasty Trust to remove assets from the gross estate entirely

Appendix C — Income Tax Reduction on Sale

QSBS §1202 & Donor Advised Fund Strategy  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

QSBS — §1202 Overview

Section 1202 of the Internal Revenue Code allows non-corporate taxpayers to exclude up to 100% of gain on Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) held for more than 5 years. For Delaware C-Corp founders, this is often the single most valuable tax provision available — potentially eliminating millions in federal tax.

NYS Conformity — 2026 Update

NYS Conforms to QSBS (as of June 2026). The 2026 NY Budget included a proposal to de-conform from §1202 QSBS exclusions. This proposal was debated and ultimately voted down — NYS continues to conform to federal QSBS treatment. This means the NY State income tax exclusion also applies at exit — additional savings of roughly $1.09M at the 10.9% NYS rate on a full $10M exclusion. Monitor annually — de-conform risk remains in future budget cycles.

DAF — Donor Advised Fund Strategy

A Donor Advised Fund (DAF) allows a founder to make a tax-deductible charitable contribution of appreciated stock before a liquidity event, avoiding capital gains entirely and receiving an immediate deduction.

  • Timing: contribute QSBS shares to the DAF before any sale agreement is signed
  • Deduction: full FMV deduction up to 30% of AGI; 5-year carryforward for excess
  • Gain avoidance: the DAF is tax-exempt — no capital gains on appreciated stock inside the DAF
  • Grant flexibility: grants to IRS-qualified charities over time; no payout deadline
  • Minimum contribution: most major DAF sponsors (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard) accept $5,000+
  • QSBS interaction: contributing pre-exclusion shares to the DAF creates a double benefit — exclusion + deduction

Appendix D — Dynasty Trust

Delaware Dynasty Trust for the Founder's Descendants  ·  Illustrative Structure  ·  2026

What Is the Dynasty Trust?

The Dynasty Trust is an irrevocable trust established to hold an independent slice of the company — illustrated here at 5% — for the benefit of the founder's descendants (children, current or future). Delaware situs is recommended for its favorable trust law (Appendix E) and its abolition of the Rule Against Perpetuities, allowing the trust to benefit multiple generations without a forced termination date.

Key Structural Features

  • Situs: Delaware — no state income tax on undistributed trust income; strong privacy laws
  • Irrevocable — assets removed from the founder's estate; spendthrift provisions protect from creditors
  • Beneficiaries: the founder's descendants — a true multi-generational, dynasty structure
  • Flexible distributions — trustee has discretion over income/principal distributions to beneficiaries
  • Grantor trust option — founder can pay trust income taxes personally, an additional tax-free gift at 38.6% (Appendix G)

QSBS Shelter at the Illustrative Exit

At the illustrative $75M exit, the Dynasty Trust's 5% slice is worth $3,750,000. Because this is well under the trust's own $10,000,000 QSBS exclusion cap, the entire trust gain can be sheltered from federal (and NYS-conforming) capital gains tax once QSBS qualification is confirmed — no multi-trust structuring is required at this exit size.

Note: at a larger exit valuation, if the trust's share of proceeds were to exceed the $10M single-trust cap, splitting into multiple sub-trusts — one per beneficiary — can multiply the available exclusion. T&E counsel can model this if and when it becomes relevant.

Trustee Selection

  • Independent individual trustee — flexibility, personal relationship, lower cost; succession risk
  • Institutional / corporate trustee (bank trust dept.) — continuity, expertise, regulated; higher cost
  • Directed trust structure — institutional administrative trustee + investment advisor (Allegiance) as advisor
  • Trust Protector — third party with power to modify trust, change trustees, or adapt to law changes

Beneficiary Designations & Funding Timing

Beneficiaries: the founder's descendants — children, current or future. As a true dynasty structure, the trust is designed to benefit not only the first generation but their descendants as well, taking advantage of Delaware's abolition of the Rule Against Perpetuities.

Funding timing: gifting the trust's 5% slice now at today's ~$15M valuation uses only $750,000 of lifetime exemption, versus $3,750,000 if gifted at the $75M exit valuation instead — funding early is one of the highest-value planning actions available.

Action: confirm structure and drafting with T&E counsel before funding.

Appendix E — Trust Situs

Delaware as Optimal Trust Jurisdiction  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

Why Delaware?

Trust situs (legal jurisdiction) determines which state's laws govern the trust and whether undistributed trust income is subject to state income tax. Delaware offers the most favorable combination of tax treatment, legal flexibility, privacy, and duration for the Dynasty Trust.

Delaware Advantages

No State Income Tax
DE does not tax undistributed income of a trust with no DE-resident beneficiaries. The Dynasty Trust avoids both NY State (10.9%) and NYC (3.876%) income tax at the trust level.
No Rule Against Perpetuities
DE abolished the RAP in 1995. Trusts can last for a very long duration. New York, by contrast, has an 85-year RAP.
Directed Trust Statute
DE allows bifurcation of trustee duties — separate investment advisor (Allegiance) and administrative trustee. Maximizes flexibility and professional management.
Asset Protection
Strong spendthrift and creditor protection provisions. Trust assets insulated from beneficiary creditors, divorce claims, and judgments.
Privacy
DE court records relating to trusts are not public. No public registration of trust terms required.
Legal Sophistication
DE has the most developed body of trust law; Delaware Court of Chancery provides expert trust dispute resolution.

Appendix G — Grantor vs. Non-Grantor Trusts

Income Tax Treatment  ·  SLAT & SLANT as Examples  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

Grantor vs. Non-Grantor Trust — Income Tax Treatment

Every irrevocable trust is classified as either a Grantor Trust or a Non-Grantor Trust for income tax purposes. This classification determines who pays the income tax on trust earnings — and has profound implications for how quickly the trust corpus compounds (see Appendix M).

Grantor Trust
The founder pays income tax on ALL trust income personally, at their own rate (38.6% NYC). The trust itself pays no tax. Each tax payment by the founder is effectively an additional tax-free gift to the trust — supercharging growth. The trust corpus compounds at the full gross return (6.5%).
Non-Grantor Trust
The trust is a separate taxpayer. It pays its own income tax on earnings at trust tax rates. The trust corpus compounds at the net after-tax return (~4.0% after a 38.6% drag on 6.5% gross). No additional depletion of the founder's personal estate from tax payments.
The Trade-Off
Grantor Trust: faster corpus growth, additional estate depletion via tax payments — best for founders with outside liquidity to absorb the tax bill. Non-Grantor: simpler, self-contained, still outside the estate. See Appendix M for the 45-year consolidated family wealth comparison.

SLAT & SLANT — Spousal Trust Examples (Future Planning Upon Marriage)

SLATs and SLANTs are irrevocable trusts that include a spouse as a lifetime beneficiary. They are presented here as examples of how Grantor/Non-Grantor classification interacts with spousal access. These are future structures — not relevant until marriage. The Dynasty Trust (Appendix D) is established now, before marriage, for the founder's descendants, without spousal provisions.

SLAT — Spousal Lifetime Access Trust
  • Structure: irrevocable trust; grantor spouse transfers assets; beneficiary spouse has discretionary access
  • QSBS: LOSES §1202 exclusion — trust treated as grantor trust; gain taxed to grantor at time of sale
  • Use case: married couples prioritizing spousal access over QSBS optimization
SLANT — Spousal Lifetime Access Non-Grantor Trust
  • Structure: SLAT with 'adverse party' consent required for distributions — breaks grantor trust status
  • QSBS: PRESERVES §1202 exclusion — non-grantor treatment means the trust qualifies as a separate taxpayer
  • Use case: married couples who want spousal access AND to preserve QSBS eligibility

Appendix J — Charitable Giving Vehicles

DAF, CRUT, Foundation  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

Overview

A liquidity event is an optimal moment to establish a charitable giving strategy. The combination of a large capital gain, QSBS exclusion, and anticipated family growth creates multiple planning opportunities. Vehicles range from simple (DAF) to sophisticated (private foundation).

Donor Advised Fund (DAF)

  • Simplest vehicle — contribution is irrevocable; grants recommended over time
  • Deduction: FMV of assets contributed; avoids capital gains on appreciated stock
  • Minimum contribution: $5,000 (Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, others)
  • No annual payout requirement — flexible grant timing
  • Best for: immediate deduction at exit + ongoing philanthropy

Charitable Remainder Unitrust (CRUT)

  • Contribute appreciated stock / QSBS — no immediate capital gains tax
  • Trust pays out a fixed % (commonly ~5%) of corpus annually to the founder for life or a term of years
  • Remainder to charity at termination — partially deductible at contribution
  • Income stream diversifies the founder's cash flow post-exit
  • Best for: income need + charitable intent + a large appreciated position

Private Foundation

  • Full control over grant-making; family members can serve as officers
  • Deduction limited to 30% AGI (cash) / 20% AGI (appreciated property)
  • 5% annual distribution requirement; excise taxes on investment income
  • Best for: significant philanthropic intent ($5M+), family legacy, grant-making control
  • Higher administrative cost; subject to self-dealing rules
Recommended: DAF at Exit + CRUT for a Large QSBS Position. Establish a DAF immediately at exit — fund with $1–2M of non-QSBS appreciated stock. Consider a CRUT for a portion of QSBS excess (above the $10M exclusion) to avoid partial capital gain. A private foundation is deferred — consider at a $5M+ philanthropic commitment level post-exit.

Appendix M — Estate Freeze Analysis

Consolidated Family Wealth  ·  45-Year Projection to Life Expectancy  ·  Illustrative Example  ·  2026

What Is an Estate Freeze — and Why Does It Matter?

An estate freeze transfers appreciating assets into the Dynasty Trust today, so that all future growth accrues outside the taxable estate. The question is not just 'how big does the trust get?' — it is 'how much does the family receive in total, after estate tax, compared to doing nothing?' The three scenarios below compare all assets on a consolidated basis from the same starting point.

Same starting capital in all cases: Founder personal proceeds $15,000,000 + Dynasty Trust proceeds (5% of a $75M exit) $3,750,000 = $18,750,000. Founder age at exit: 37 · Life expectancy: 82 (SSA actuarial default) · 45 years of compounding.

Gross return: 6.5% · Combined income tax rate: 38.6% · Net after-tax: ~3.99% · Grantor Trust: 6.5% (full gross) · Estate tax: Federal 40% (above $15M exemption) + NY ~16% (cliff triggered on estates this size) — illustrative combined rate 50%, adjustable by state in the Founder Exit Toolkit's Post Exit Tax Planning calculator.
